Connectome analysis of a cerebellum-like 
circuit for sensory prediction
Krista E. Perks1,5, Mariela D. Petkova2,5, Salomon Z. Muller1,5 ✉, Michael Genecin1, 
Adishree Ghatare1, Richard Schalek2, Yuelong Wu2, Michal Januszewski3, Viren Jain4, 
Jeff W. Lichtman2, L. F. Abbott1 & Nathaniel B. Sawtell1 ✉
Many forms of learning, for example, learning a model of the environment or a motor 
skill, rely on synaptic plasticity that is widely distributed across cell types and network 
stages. Understanding how this distributed plasticity functions is a central challenge 
in neuroscience1–5. Here we use connectomics to map the cell types and synaptic 
connections underlying a form of multi-layer continual learning that cancels 
predictable sensory responses in a cerebellum-like structure in electric fish6,7.  
Our analysis shows inhibitory and disinhibitory sensory input pathways that fulfil 
theoretical requirements for instructing synaptic plasticity8,9, structured synaptic 
connectivity between network stages that solves a credit assignment problem and 
structured recurrent connectivity that accelerates sensory prediction and cancellation. 
A computational model constrained by electrophysiological recordings shows  
how this synaptic connectivity ensures that multiple sites of plasticity cooperate to 
overcome their individual limitations, resulting in cancellation that is fast, accurate 
and robust to noise. Overall, these findings highlight the potential of connectomics,  
in combination with cell-type-specific physiological recordings and computational 
modelling, for deciphering learning in neural circuits.
High-resolution analysis of neural circuits using connectomics has 
led to important advances in understanding neural computations, 
ranging from direction selectivity in the retina to spatial navigation 
in insects 10,11. However, as connectomics provides a static view of 
neural circuitry, its utility for investigating learning remains to be 
established. Here, we use connectomics, in tight combination with 
neural recordings and computational modelling, to explain the neu-
ral basis for a form of internal model learning in the electrosensory 
lobe (ELL) of African pulse-type electric fish. The ELL is particularly 
amenable to analysis using serial-section electron microscopy (EM) 
because its main cell and input types form a repeating microcircuit 
with local connectivity restricted to a small volume12,13. Furthermore, 
these cell and input types have been the subject of extensive electro-
physiological investigations8,14,15 and can be identified based solely 
on their morphology16–21. As well as providing an interesting exam-
ple of the use of learned predictions in sensing (see below), the ELL 
shares numerous features of its circuitry and physiology with the  
cerebellum22.
African weakly electric fish emit brief (<0.5 ms duration) electrical 
pulses, known as electric organ discharges (EODs), for communica-
tion and active sensing in low-visibility habitats23 (Fig. 1a). These fish 
also sense weak electric fields generated by prey using specialized, 
low-frequency-tuned electroreceptors on their skin24. EODs induce 
damped oscillatory responses in these electroreceptors that mask 
prey signals (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, these responses are modulated 
by environmental factors, such as water conductivity, that alter the 
amplitude and shape of the EOD waveform25 (Fig. 1a).
The ELL solves this interference problem by combining input 
from electroreceptors with excitatory input from granule cells (GCs; 
Fig. 1b) that transform a copy of the EOD motor command (corol -
lary discharge) into a large set of temporally diverse signals that 
span the duration of the electroreceptor response to the EOD14. GC 
synapses are subject to ongoing bi-directional synaptic plasticity 
consisting of associative synaptic depression and non-associative 
potentiation 26,27. The overall effect of these two forms of plastic -
ity is to generate temporally specific predictions, termed negative 
images, which cancel the response to the EOD by driving the post -
synaptic neuron towards a constant ‘equilibrium’ firing rate14,26,28–30  
(Fig. 1b).
Although the functional importance of negative images for enhanc-
ing neural coding and behavioural detection of prey-related signals is 
well-established15, their proposed neural implementation is complex 
(Fig. 1b). Electrophysiological recordings indicate that negative images 
are formed at multiple sites, including GC synapses onto GABAergic 
interneurons, known as medium ganglion (MG) cells, and onto glu-
tamatergic Output cells 8,26,28,29. MG and Output cells (analogues of 
Purkinje cells and cerebellar nucleus cells, respectively) are further 
divided into morphologically distinct subtypes with opposite polar-
ity responses to electrosensory stimuli 13 (MG+, MG−, ON and OFF;

T o accomplish this, we used serial-section EM to reconstruct the ELL 
microcircuit (Fig. 1c,d and Supplementary Video 1). This analysis con-
firmed two main predictions of previous models of ELL function8,9. First, 
the main sensory input to MG and Output cells is not relayed directly 
by excitatory electroreceptor afferent nerve fibres (EAFs) but rather 
through inhibitory and disinhibitory pathways mediated by two stages 
of interneurons (Gr+, SP+) and (SP−, SG−) (Fig. 1c). These pathways 
fulfil important biophysical requirements for instructing synaptic 
plasticity in MG cells8,9. Second, connectivity between MG and Output 
cell subtypes is extremely precise and matches that predicted if nega-
tive images form within MG cells and cancel EOD-evoked responses in 
Output cells8 (Fig. 1b,c). Connectome analysis also showed new features 
of ELL circuitry, including highly structured recurrent connections 
between MG cell subtypes (Fig. 1c). Incorporating these connections 
into a detailed computational model of the ELL recapitulated meas-
ured neuronal responses and suggests an important role for recur-
rent circuitry in accelerating learning. Finally, modelling and in vivo 
recordings demonstrate an important functional role for dual sites of 
plasticity in MG and Output cells under realistic scenarios in which the 
ELL must maintain accurate negative images in the presence of noise 
from external sources, including the EODs of conspecifics (Fig. 1a).
Serial-section EM reconstruction of ELL circuitry
We generated a 450 × 450 × 105 µm imaged volume of the ELL using 
serial-section EM31 (Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 1a,b; see also Sup-
plementary Video 1). Automated segmentation was performed using 
a flood filling network pipeline32, which was retrained for the ELL vol-
ume, followed by manual synapse annotation (Methods). Consistent 
with previous EM studies of electric fish, ultrastructure and synapse 
morphology resembled that of other vertebrates, including typical 
‘asymmetric’ (putative excitatory) and ‘symmetric’ (putative inhibi-
tory) synapses33,34 (Fig. 1d,f and Extended Data Fig. 1c–e; see also Sup-
plementary Video 2). The main cell types of the ELL, including ON, 
OFF , MG+ and MG− cells, could all be unambiguously classified based 
on morphological features (Fig. 1e,f and Extended Data Fig. 2; see also 
Methods and Extended Data Table 1). Iterative annotation and recon-
struction of MG cell synaptic targets resulted in a densely reconstructed 
network of 88 Output and 136 MG cells. Tracing the input pathways to 
these two main cell types resulted in the sparse reconstruction of three 
additional interneuron classes: small ganglion (SG), granular (Gr+), 
and small plexiform (SP−) cells (Fig. 1g,h and Extended Data Fig. 2; 
see also Extended Data Table 1 and Methods). Similar to MG cells, SG 
cells are divided into subtypes referred to as SG+ and SG− based on 
their axonal and dendritic morphologies (Extended Data Fig. 2c–f). 
Although the in vivo response properties of SG, GR+, and SP− cells 
are largely uncharacterized35, their synapse morphology along with 
previous immunohistochemical and EM studies indicate that they 
are inhibitory12,13,17,19 (Extended Data Fig. 1). In all, our analysis includes 
29,694 annotated synapses and 5,016 reconstructed neurons (out of 
about 10,000 total neurons in the volume).
The GC input pathway
GC inputs to the molecular layer of the ELL convey electric organ corol-
lary discharges (as well as additional information) that provide the basis 
for predicting self-generated sensory input to the ELL14,36 (Fig. 1b–d). GC 
axons form synapses onto dendritic spines of Output, MG and SG cells 
as well as onto smooth, beaded dendrites of molecular layer interneu-
rons and other interneuron types (Extended Data Fig. 3a). No clear pat-
terns were observed in the connectivity of individual GC axons (n = 64) 
onto Output, MG and SG subtypes (Extended Data Fig. 3b). However, 
given the small fraction of GC axons analysed here, we cannot rule out 
biases in GC connectivity. We estimated the relative proportion of GC 
input received by different ELL cell types using spine count as a proxy 
(Methods). On average, individual SG cells have many fewer spines 
(482), than Output (5,163) or MG cells (15,811). However, there are far 
more SG than MG or Output cells. Collectively, Output cells and SG 
cells receive roughly the same amount of GC input, whereas MG cells 
receive fivefold greater GC input (Extended Data Fig. 3c). Although 
little is known at present regarding the physiological responses and 
plasticity of SG cells, we explore their possible functions in the model-
ling sections below.
Finally, apart from GC axons, we also found thicker fibres that prob-
ably correspond to a previously described excitatory feedback projec-
tion from a higher stage of electrosensory processing37,38. Although this 
projection has important roles in sensory cancellation in the active 
electrosensory system of South American weakly electric fish39–41, we 
do not discuss it further here as it has not been electrophysiologically 
characterized in the context of passive electrolocation.
Electrosensory input pathways
Our next goal was to map the electrosensory input pathways to the ELL. 
Similar to cerebellar Purkinje cells, MG cells exhibit two types of action 
potentials: narrow spikes (which are axonal) constitute their main sig-
nalling output, whereas broad spikes (which are dendritic) induce 
synaptic plasticity26,29. Previous work has shown that electrosensory 
stimuli modulate broad spike firing rate with little impact on narrow 
spikes and that this selectivity is vital for the axonal transmission of 
negative images to Output cells8 (Extended Data Fig. 4a). A biophysical 
model indicated that this selectivity can be explained if electrosensory 
input is relayed by inhibition (for MG−) and disinhibition (for MG+)8,9. 
The prediction of a disinhibitory (rather than directly excitatory) path-
way for sensory input onto MG+ cells is notable because EAFs termi-
nate near the basal dendrites of MG+ cells and hence are anatomically 
well-positioned to excite them directly.
T o trace electrosensory input pathways, we chose example ON, OFF , 
MG+, and MG− cells and reconstructed the cells synapsing onto their 
basal dendrites, soma and axon (Fig. 2a,b). This analysis excludes syn-
apses from GCs and MLIs (which are the main inputs onto the apical 
dendrites) and synapses from MG cells (which are discussed below). 
The different cell types of the ELL, including those chosen here for 
reconstruction of presynaptic inputs, are intermixed in the x- and 
z-dimensions of the volume without any apparent spatial pattern 
(Fig. 2b).
Putative excitatory synapses from EAFs made up only a small fraction 
of the input to ON and MG+ cells (Fig. 2c–f). The main input to all MG 
and Output subtypes arose instead from the SG+, SG−, Gr+, and SP− 
inhibitory interneuron classes (Fig. 2c–f). A strictly segregated pattern 
of connectivity was observed in which ON and MG+ cells received syn-
apses from SG−, and SP− and EAFs, whereas OFF and MG− cells received 
synapses from SG+ and Gr+ cells (Fig. 2g,h and Extended Data Fig. 5).
Proceeding backwards along the electrosensory pathway, we recon-
structed presynaptic neurons synapsing onto SG+, SG−, SP− and Gr+ 
cells (Fig. 3a). Again, a segregated pattern of connectivity was observed 
in which SG− and SP− cells received their main input from Gr+ cells, 
whereas SG+ cells received both EAF and SG− input (Fig.  3b–g and 
Extended Data Fig. 5). Finally, Gr+ cells received their main input from 
EAFs (Fig. 3c–e,g and Extended Data Fig. 5).
Summarizing these connectivity patterns, two stages of inhibition 
in series preserve the sign of EAF input to ON and MG+ cells, whereas 
a single stage of inhibition inverts the sign of EAF input to OFF and 
MG− cells (Fig. 1c). Thus, the connectome results provide not only an 
anatomical basis for the distinct ON and OFF responses to sensory input 
observed in Output and MG cell subtypes but also striking support for 
the proposed disinhibitory and inhibitory effects of electrosensory 
input on MG cells9.

one synapse violated the MG− to ON, MG+ to OFF pattern. Similarly, of 
1,322 MG-to-MG synapses, only 10 were between similar MG subtypes. 
This is a testament both to the accuracy of synaptic targeting in the ELL 
and to the quality of our connectome reconstruction. Although the 
spatial overlap between MG cell axons and Output cell somas suggest 
a rough wiring selectivity8, it cannot account for the nearly perfect 
wiring specificity demonstrated here. Additional connectome analysis 
revealed that MG synapses are not restricted to Output cell somas but 
also synapse heavily on their basal dendrites (Fig. 4f,g). Similarly, the 
specificity of the recurrent connections between MG subtypes cannot 
be inferred solely from the spatial overlap between their axons and basal 
dendrites. A substantial fraction of MG recurrent synapses target the 
somas of MG+ and MG− cells (Fig. 4f) both of which are located in the 
ganglion layer (Fig. 4h and Extended Data Fig. 2h,i).
We also examined the convergence of MG cells onto Output and other 
MG cells and found a median of 7 MGs converging onto one Output cell 
and a 3:1 convergence ratio for MGs onto each other (median values; 
Fig. 4i and Extended Data Fig. 6c). As far as divergence, an MG cell syn-
apses onto six Output cells and five other MGs (median values; Fig. 4i 
and Extended Data Fig. 6c). These are probably underestimates due 
to the truncation of MG cell axonal arbours by the EM volume bound-
ary. Connection probability declined with distance, consistent with 
synaptically connected MG and Output cells sharing electrosensory 
input from the same region of the body surface (Fig. 4j).
Structured synaptic connectivity supports learning
Having mapped the synaptic connectivity of the ELL in detail, we 
now address its functional significance in predicting and cancelling 
self-generated interference due to the EOD. T o this end, we constructed 
a model that incorporates the synaptic connectivity shown by the con-
nectome and uses measured EAF and GC responses and measured 
synaptic plasticity at GC synapses onto MG and Output cells (Meth-
ods). The model is further constrained by matching data from in vivo 
electrophysiological recordings of morphologically identified MG 
and Output cells, including the finding that sensory input strongly 
modulates MG broad spike rate with little impact on narrow spikes by 
regulating axonal spike backpropagation8,9. For modelling recurrent 
inhibition (the effects of which have not been directly measured), we 
adopt the standard assumption that inhibition modulates both broad 
and narrow spike rates through membrane potential hyperpolarization 
(Extended Data Fig. 4c,d). SG cells have not been included in previous 
models of the ELL. Our connectome results indicate several possible 
roles for them; for example, because at least some SG cells receive GC 
input, they could take part in negative image learning. We explored this 
possibility in our model, assuming that GC to SG synapses exhibit the 
same plasticity as those onto MG and Output cells. Plasticity in indi-
vidual SG cells is slow because of the small number of GC synapses they 
receive (Extended Data Fig. 3c), making their impact on cancellation 
negligible in the model (Extended Data Fig. 4e). For this reason, and 
because no recordings from identified SG cells are currently available, 
the modelling in this section treats SG cells purely as sensory relays. 
Because SG+ and Gr+ cells share similar connectivity patterns, as do 
SG− and SP− cells (Figs. 1c and 3), these cell pairs are combined as single 
functional units in the model presented here (Fig. 5a). An alternative 
hypothesis regarding SG cells is considered in the Discussion.
We simulated previous experiments (Methods) in which cancellation 
was monitored using in vivo electrophysiological recordings (Fig. 5a). 
In these experiments, fish continue to generate EOD motor commands 
(engaging the GC input to the ELL shown in Fig. 1b), but the emission of 
the EOD itself is blocked by a paralytic. Under these conditions, ongo-
ing plasticity drives MG cell broad spiking and Output cell spiking to 
a constant (unmodulated) equilibrium rate. Then, an EOD mimic is 
triggered on the EOD commands (simulating a naturally occurring 
increase in EOD amplitude). Cancellation is quantified based on the 
decrease in MG cell broad spike or Output cell spike rate modulations 
driven by the EOD mimic. Under these conditions, both model and data 
show a rapid decrease in MG+ broad spike rate modulation and a slower 
decrease in ON cell spike rate modulation (Fig. 5b,c). It is difficult to 
detect cancellation in the spiking responses of MG− and OFF cells under 
these conditions due to rectification (which conceals subthreshold 
changes; Extended Data Fig. 7b,c). However, intracellular recordings 
of OFF cells show about 1 mV reduction over 4 min of pairing from an 
initial peak response of around 2.25 mV (Extended Data Fig. 7b).
The time course of cancellation in the model closely matches the 
data (Fig. 5b,c; Extended Data Fig. 7d,e). Having verified the accuracy 
of the model, we then used it to dissect the impact of different com-
ponents of the circuit connectivity on learning. First, we considered a 
model in which MG cells do not connect to Output cells, which means 
that cancellation in Output cells relies solely on their cell-autonomous 
plasticity (dashed lines in Fig. 5d and Extended Data Fig. 4f). In this 
case, cancellation is moderately rapid in ON cells and extremely slow 
in OFF cells. We next restored the MG to Output cell synapses to the 
model, which allows MG cells to assist in cancellation by transmitting 
their negative images to the Output cells (dotted lines in Fig. 5d and 
Extended Data Fig. 4f). This greatly increases the rate of cancellation in 
OFF cells but has only a small impact in ON cells. Finally, we considered 
the fully connected model, with MG cell connections to both Output 
cells and each other (solid lines in Fig. 5d and Extended Data Fig. 4f). 
This further increases the rate of cancellation in both Output cell types, 
but especially for ON cells.
The effects on learning speed seen in Fig. 5d can largely be explained 
by the dependence of plasticity at GC to Output and MG synapses on 
pre- and postsynaptic spike rates. This, in turn, provides a logic for the 
entire pattern of MG to Output cell connectivity. As stated previously, 
this plasticity involves non-associative potentiation and associative 
depression26,27. The rate of potentiation is proportional to the presyn-
aptic, that is, GC, spike rate. The rate of depression, by contrast, is pro-
portional to the product of the GC spike rate and the postsynaptic spike 
rate (Methods). Specifically, it varies in proportion to the ratio of the 
postsynaptic spike rate (rpost) to its equilibrium value (req) (refs. 14,30). 
Changes in the EOD that require depression of GC synapses drive plas-
ticity much more rapidly than changes that require potentiation (by a 
factor of about 8 for Output cells; Extended Data Fig. 4g) because they 
increase the postsynaptic spike rate. For the cancellation shown in Fig. 5, 
ON cells require depression and OFF cells require potentiation to con-
struct their negative images. This is why cancellation is so much faster 
for ON cells than for OFF cells when Output learning is cell-autonomous 
(Fig. 5d, dashed lines). For MG cells, in which the relevant postsynaptic 
factor is the broad spike rate, the enhancement is even larger than it is 
for Output cells, about 60 (Extended Data Fig. 4g). This explains why 
the impact of including MG connections to Output cells, and thus MG 
cell plasticity (Fig. 5d, dotted lines), is so much larger for OFF cells than 
for ON cells. In this case, OFF cells receive part of their negative image 
from MG+ cells and these, similar to ON cells, use the more rapid syn-
aptic depression to build their negative images. Notably, ELL circuitry 
pairs MG and Output cells such that one member of the pair uses faster 
depression and the other uses slower potentiation. This occurs whether 
the required learning is in response to an increase in EOD amplitude,

The ELL uses one more circuit feature to speed up learning, as indi-
cated in Fig. 5d (solid lines): MG recurrence. According to the analysis 
of the previous paragraph, the cell-autonomous plasticity rate in one of 
the two MG cell types will always be faster than in the other. Recurrent 
connections enhance learning speed by transmitting negative images 
from the MG cell with faster plasticity to the MG cell with slower plastic-
ity. This explains the approximate equality of the initial cancellation 
speeds in ON and OFF cells in the fully connected model (Fig. 5d, solid 
lines). With recurrence in place, both MG cells learn more quickly than 
either of the Output cells, and thus MG learning dominates the early 
phase of cancellation.
From the standpoint of cancellation speed, one question remains: 
from our fits of the model to data, we find that only about half of the 
contribution to the total negative image in Output cells is provided 
by MG cells, with the other half coming from the plasticity of GC 
synapses directly onto the Output cells (Fig. 5c,d and Extended Data 
Fig. 4h). If this ratio can be increased, it would presumably allow MG 
cell plasticity to make cancellation even faster, while still leaving room 
for ‘fine-tuning’ by Output cell plasticity. However, as we show in the 
following section, distributing plasticity roughly equally across two 
sites provides benefits in the presence of noise.
Robustness to noise despite continual learning
Previous experimental and theoretical work, including that in the previ-
ous section, considered responses to the EOD in isolation from other 
signals. In nature, however, electrosensory input to the ELL is likely 
to contain various sources of noise that cannot be fully predicted42, 
including the EODs of other nearby electric fish43,44. Because plasticity 
in the ELL appears to operate continuously, this raises the important 
question of whether the ELL can maintain accurate predictions in the 
presence of noise and, if so, how.
We first used the model to examine continual learning under noisy 
conditions using EOD-like pulses of different amplitudes and polarities 
to simulate other electric fish at various locations (Fig. 6a and Meth-
ods). We explored different durations of noise exposure (from 10 min 
to 100 min) and different temporal patterns, including cases in which 
the timing of the noise pulses was either random or correlated with the 
spontaneously emitted EOD commands of fish at an approximately 
16 ms delay. This latter case mimics a prominent electrocommunication 
behaviour known as the echo response45. For all scenarios tested, noise 
exposure minimally affected the accuracy of self-EOD cancellation in 
Output cells (Fig. 6b and Extended Data Fig. 8b). Importantly, this robust-
ness to noise depended strongly on plasticity occurring at both Output 
and MG cell synapses from GC cells (Fig. 6b and Extended Data Fig. 8b).
We were surprised by the robustness shown by the model and there-
fore examined what happens in analogous electrophysiological experi-
ments. Following a 10-min bout of temporally correlated (echo-like) 
pulses with random amplitude and polarity, we found that cancellation 
was as robust in real ON and OFF cells as it was in the model (Fig. 6b).
Before using the model to investigate the source of this robust -
ness, we verified that the neural responses evoked by noise in the 
data matched those observed in the model. The noise input we used 
was zero mean and evokes, on average, a strong initial increase in the 
model MG broad spike rate that is nearly identical in MG+ and MG− sub-
types (Fig. 6c). This occurs due to rectification: sensory input fluctua-
tions that increase the spike rate have a much larger effect than those 
that decrease it. The initial noise-evoked increase in broad spike rate 
drives synaptic depression at GC to MG synapses, resulting in a gradual

Although noise effects spiking and plasticity in a similar way in all four 
cell types (MG+, MG−, ON and OFF), the connectivity of the circuit 
ensures that Output cells receive two opposing cancellation signals, 
the signal they construct from their GC input and the inverted signal 
that they receive from their partner MG cells (Fig. 6d and Extended 
Data Fig. 8d). These two contributions largely cancel each other out 
(Fig. 6d), leading to a much smaller impact on Output cells than would 
be produced if the ELL had only a single stage of plasticity in MG cells 
(Fig. 6b,e and Extended Data Fig. 8e).

section as to why do MG cells contribute only around one-half of the 
total negative image in Output cells. T o address this, we examined how 
noise robustness depends on model parameters that determine this 
fraction, specifically the strength of MG to Output and MG recurrent 
connections and the rate of plasticity in Output cells. For both param-
eters, we found that values fit to previous data for self-EOD cancellation 
(that is, roughly equal contributions of MG and Output cell plasticity to 
cancellation) were close to optimal in the presence of noise (Fig. 6f,g 
and Extended Data Fig. 8f–h).

physiological recordings and modelling, the present study shows how a 
detailed knowledge of synaptic connectivity is essential for understand-
ing how several sites of synaptic plasticity support learning that is fast, 
accurate and robust to noise. Much of the detailed structure shown by 
our connectome analysis can be understood as assuring that cells with 
fast and slow plasticity are always paired, so that the fast plasticity can 
compensate for the slow. Assuring that cells with fast and slow plasticity 
are paired regardless of whether cancellation primarily involves synap-
tic potentiation or depression, requires all of the following prominent 
features of ELL circuitry: ON and OFF pathways, synaptic plasticity at 
two stages of each pathway, ‘crossed’ connectivity between MG and 
Output cells, and a strict pattern of recurrent connectivity between 
MG cell subtypes. By extending our analysis to more challenging and 
realistic social scenarios in which negative images must be maintained 
in the presence of the EOD of conspecifics, we found that having two 
separate stages of synaptic plasticity also confers robustness to noise. 
Although the idea that multiple stages of plasticity could support con-
tinual learning is not new48–50, the present work is unique in providing 
the actual implementation details at the level of specific cell types and 
synaptic connectivity patterns.
Similar to cerebellar Purkinje cells, two spike types under largely 
independent control are required for MG cells to learn negative images 
(based on ongoing plasticity of GC input) and simultaneously trans-
mit them to downstream neurons. In the case of Purkinje cells, this 
independent control is due to a specialized excitatory synaptic input 
to Purkinje cells—the powerful climbing fibre input from the inferior 
olive. Our connectome analysis showed specialized inhibitory and 
disinhibitory electrosensory input pathways to MG cells that meet 
the biophysical requirements for independent control of broad spikes 
established by previous multi-compartment models8,9. Hence, despite 
differences in their anatomical form, sensory input to the ELL and climb-
ing fibre input to the cerebellum may perform analogous functions.
Disinhibitory sensory input to MG+ cells is also essential for allowing 
recurrent connections to accelerate learning. Previous biophysical 
modelling and recordings suggest that if sensory input to MG+ cells 
was relayed primarily through direct EAF excitation (as might have been 
expected based on the spatial overlap of EAF terminals and MG+ basal 
dendrites), both broad and narrow spike firing would be modulated by 
the EOD8,9. Our model shows that recurrent connections amplify the 
unwanted effects of the EOD in this scenario, impeding cancellation 
(Extended Data Fig. 4a). These findings illustrate the importance of 
combining detailed anatomical and physiological analyses for infer-
ring circuit function. Another broader implication is that recurrent 
inhibition may accelerate learning in other circuits in which it has  
been described, including the cerebral cortex51, hippocampus52,53 and 
cerebellum54,55.
SG cells represent a prominent site of integration of sensory and 
predictive information (GC input), the function of which remains to 
be established. Both SG and MG cells exhibit substantial variation in 
size, including the length of their basal and apical dendritic arbours 
and their soma diameter (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b,g). A possibility is 
that this variation is related to the fact that the mormyrid brain exhibits 
growth throughout life, including substantial enlargement of the ELL 
and continuous addition of adult-born GCs56. One hypothesis is that 
the MG and SG cell classes actually represent a continuum (Extended 
Data Fig. 2b), with SG cells slowly maturing into MG cells even in adults13. 
For SG cells to take on the function of MG cells, our model predicts 
that their connectivity must change in a specific way: disinhibitory SG 
input should increase relative to excitatory EAF input during matura-
tion. This hypothesis could be explored in our existing EM volume by 
comparing the electrosensory input pathways for SG and MG cells as 
a function of their size.
Finally, an in-depth analysis of the synaptic connectivity of the molec-
ular layer of the ELL is likely to provide additional insight, with particular 
relevance to learning in cerebellar circuits22. Key questions include: 
the connectivity of MLIs and whether they comprise functionally and 
anatomically distinct sub-classes, as in the cerebellum55; whether GC 
input to synaptically coupled MG and Output cells is primarily shared 
(as our model assumes) or distinct; and whether anatomical correlates 
of plasticity at GC synapses can be identified.

Methods
Experimental model and subject details
Wild-caught fish (7–14 cm in length) of undetermined sex of the spe-
cies Gnathonemus petersii were used in these experiments. Fish were 
housed in 60-gallon tanks in groups of 5–20. Water conductivity was 
maintained between 40 and 65 microsiemens both in the home tanks 
of the fish and during experiments. All experiments performed in this 
study adhere to the Guiding Principles in the Care and Use of Animals of 
the American Physiological Society and were approved by the Institu-
tional Animal Care and Use Committee of Columbia University.
EM data acquisition and processing
Two fish of the species G. petersii were deeply anaesthetized with tric-
aine methanesulfonate (MS:222, 1:10,000) and transcardially perfused 
with 2% paraformaldehyde and 2% glutaraldehyde in 0.15 M sodium 
cacodylate buffer. Following perfusion, 2.5 mm × 2 mm × 0.4 mm vol-
umes of the hindbrain containing the anterior ELL were processed 
for EM using a modified rOTO staining protocol57. The size of the skin 
region projecting to the imaged volume cannot be precisely estimated 
because of the differences in receptor density and central magnifica-
tion factor associated with different body locations. However, given 
its anterior location, the volume probably contains the central rep -
resentation of a few mm of the skin surface on the chin appendage58. 
The specimen ultimately selected for imaging was 9.3 cm long and 
weighed 6.9 g. After dissection, the tissue was washed three times 
for 10 min each in 0.15 M sodium cacodylate buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, 
C0250) at room temperature, then incubated in 2% osmium tetroxide 
(Electron Microscopy Sciences, #19190) in 0.15 M sodium cacodylate 
for 30 min flat followed by 4 h on a rotator at room temperature. The 
sample was rinsed (3 × 10 min, 0.15 M sodium cacodylate) and incu -
bated in 2.5% potassium ferrocyanide (Sigma-Aldrich, P3289) in 0.15 M 
sodium cacodylate for 2 h at room temperature, followed by overnight 
incubation at 4 °C. The next day, samples were washed with Milli-Q 
water (3 × 10 min) and incubated in freshly prepared, 0.45-µm-filtered 
1% (w/v) thiocarbohydrazide (Sigma-Aldrich, 223220) in water for 1 h 
at room temperature. Following water washes (3 × 10 min), the tissue 
was post-fixed with 2% osmium tetroxide (Electron Microscopy Sci-
ences, #19190) in water for 4 h at room temperature, washed again 
(3 × 10 min) and incubated overnight at 4 °C in 2% aqueous uranyl ace-
tate (Electron Microscopy Sciences, #22400). Samples were brought to 
room temperature the following day, placed in a 60 °C oven for 1 h, and 
returned to room temperature before final water washes (3 × 10 min). 
Dehydration was carried out in a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 75%, 
95% and 3 × 100%) using wide-mouth pipettes to avoid tissue damage. 
The tissue was infiltrated with increasing concentrations of LX-112 
resin in propylene oxide (25%, 50% and 75%) and then with 100% resin, 
freshly prepared using 29.1 g NMA, 46.8 g LX-112, 14.1 g NSA and 1.8 g 
BDMA (Ladd Research, #21212). Resin infiltration was performed over 
2 days with uncapped tubes to allow evaporation of propylene oxide. 
On the final day, the sample was embedded in BEEM capsule moulds 
and cured at 60 °C.
The resin-embedded sample was trimmed and sectioned using an 
ultramicrotome (UC6, Leica) fitted with Diatome Trim 90 and Diatome 
Ultra 45/Ultra 35 diamond knives, respectively (Diatome). A 250 µm 
depth of tissue was serially sectioned at about 30 nm thickness using an 
automated tape-collecting ultramicrotome (ATUM) system59 and col-
lected onto carbon-coated Kapton tape. Sections were mounted 120 per 
wafer across 69 silicon wafers using double-sided carbon tape. Wafers 
were post-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate before imaging 
with a multibeam scanning electron microscope (MultiSEM, Zeiss) 
as in ref. 31. Wafer overviews were first acquired using reflected light 
microscopy (Zeiss Axio Imager) to guide automated acquisition. Imag-
ing proceeded in phases using a 61-beam configuration with a 4 nm pixel 
size and a 200 ns dwell time. A custom workflow manager monitored 
acquisition, validated data integrity and focus, and generated real-time 
diagnostic reports (https://github.com/YuelongWu/mSEM_workflow_
manager). We collected images spanning a 105 µm depth, totalling 3,534 
sections, of which 83 non-consecutive sections were missing, with the 
exception of one instance with three consecutively skipped sections. 
Stitching was performed using a block-matching algorithm on a scat-
tered grid within the overlapping regions of image tiles, followed by 
elastic optimization across high-resolution tiles. The stitched images 
were then rendered into non-overlapping 4,096 × 4,096 pixel tiles. 
T o enable large-scale registration, low-resolution thumbnail images 
(512 nm pixel resolution) were generated in parallel with acquisition 
and aligned using a combination of feature-based affine registration, 
template matching and spring-mesh relaxation. The transformations 
resulting from this thumbnail alignment were then upscaled and used 
as an initial estimation for fine alignment. The fine alignment process 
involved its own template matching and spring-mesh relaxation steps, 
but at a much higher resolution (16 nm) compared with the thumb -
nails. Finally, the resulting alignment transformations were applied 
to the full-resolution stitched dataset. The stitching and the align -
ment processes were carried out with a combination of feabas (https://
github.com/YuelongWu/feabas) and mb_aligner (https://github.com/ 
adisuissa/mb_aligner).
EM segmentation and skeletonization
We first applied 16 nm and 32 nm flood-filling networks (FFNs), origi-
nally trained on a human cortical dataset31,32, to the ELL dataset to gener-
ate a preliminary segmentation. This preliminary output was manually 
agglomerated to create a ground truth dataset consisting of complete 
axons and dendrites. Using this ground truth, we retrained the original 
human cortex FFNs, and used sparse point pair annotations to select 
optimal model checkpoints. This yielded optimized 16 nm and 32 nm 
FFN models, which were used to produce a base segmentation tuned 
for low merge error and an agglomeration that reduced split errors. 
At the agglomeration stage, we applied soma splitting using 12,700 
manually annotated soma using VAST60. Skeletons were then generated 
from the base segmentation using the same TEASAR-based method 
used for the human cortical dataset.
Cellular reconstruction proofreading
After base segmentation and agglomeration of the EM volume were 
completed, a modified version of CREST was used to reconstruct all 
neurons and neuron fragments in the current dataset 31. Complete 
instructions and a demonstration of the proofreading process in CREST 
is available at https://github.com/ashapsoncoe/CREST. The modified 
CREST codebase (eCREST) used in this study is available at https://
github.com/neurologic/efish_em_ELL. When proofreading a biological 
object using CREST, a reconstruction was initiated from a seed base 
segment and all base segments that were agglomerated with it. Split 
errors were corrected by adding on missing base segments (and their 
agglomerated base segments) and merge errors were corrected by 
removing base segments that did not belong to the object (which was 
made more efficient by CREST). An object was considered complete 
once all base segments that define its morphology were included, 
and base segments that were not part of it were excluded. An exam-
ple of the relationship between these stages of proofreading is avail-
able at https://neuroglancer-demo.appspot.com/#!gs://efish-public/
ng_states/Proofreading_example.json. Correction of merge errors 
within an individual base segment was not possible. CREST enabled all 
base segments in each branch of a neuron to be labelled according to 
their subcellular compartment (axon, apical dendrite, basal dendrite 
and soma; Supplemental Video 1). This ability used the igraph library 
(https://python.igraph.org/en/stable/). When a single base segment 
spanned multiple structures, it was categorized as ‘multiple’ . For each 
proofread neuron object, the identified and labelled final set of base 
segments was recorded into JSON state, which could be reloaded in 
eCREST for annotation (of synapses and soma), inspection and/or 
further proofreading.
Morphological identification
Cells that receive plastic GC input from the molecular layer are dis-
tinguished by spiny apical dendrites—these include two classes that 
have been characterized anatomically and electrophysiologically: 
Output cells and MG cells12,13,16–21 (Extended Data Table 1). These two 
classes have the largest soma of cells ELL, with output cells tending to 
be larger than MG cells (Extended Data Fig. 2g). MG cells are distinct 
from Output cells in that the former are interneurons, synapsing locally 
within the ELL, whereas the axons of Output cells exit the ELL without 
forming collaterals. The morphology of the basal dendrites of MG and 
Output cells are also highly distinct (Fig. 1e,f). A third class of cells with 
spiny apical dendrites has been previously identified morphologically 
are the SG cells13,19 (Extended Data Table 1). Like the larger MG cells, SG 
cells have spiny apical dendrites and axons that terminate locally within 
the ELL. Although it is possible that SG and MG cells form a continuum, 
for consistency with previous work and to aid the present analysis 
we objectively split these classes using hierarchical clustering (Ward 
method with Euclidean distance) based on the following morphologi-
cal criteria: soma size, lateral spread of basal dendrite and axon (along 
x- and z-axes), and apical dendritic size (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). Point 
annotation classes in Neuroglancer (implemented using eCREST) were 
used to mark and measure the diameter and location of the soma of 
each neuron. We used the statistics of the x, y, z locations of the set of 
skeleton nodes (from the base segment skeletonization) for all base 
segments in each structure for each cell to quantify the basal dendrite 
and axon spread (std in x and y) and apical dendrite size (node count). 
There were 59/728 MG and SG cells that were manually identified as 
having either dendrites or axons out of the volume, or soma partially 
out of the volume could not be included in the hierarchical clustering. 
These cells were classified manually based on the remaining parts of 
the cells that were within the volume.
Output cell subtypes (ON and OFF) are distinguishable by soma 
positions in the plexiform and ganglion layers, respectively (Extended 
Data Table 1 and Extended Data Fig. 2h). ON cells also tend to have 
a more fusiform soma shape than OFF cells. We used the basal den -
drite location of the MG+/MG− subtypes to assist in defining a bound-
ary plane between the ganglion and plexiform layers (Fig.  3b and 
Extended Data Fig. 2c). Two Output cells had soma intersecting the 
ganglion-plexiform boundary and were categorized by their soma 
shape (fusiform compared with round) (Fig. 3b). The basal dendrites 
of MG+ cells arborize in the plexiform layer, whereas those of MG− cells 
arborize in the ganglion layer (Extended Data Fig. 2c). The axons of 
MG+ and MG− cells have the opposite pattern: MG+ type arborize in 
the ganglion layer, whereas MG− type arborize predominantly in the 
plexiform layer (although they also send terminals into the ganglion 
layer). This pattern is consistent with the type 1 and 2 morphological 
characterization previously established for MG cells (MG−:MG1 and 
MG+:MG2). The anti-correlated relationship between axon and basal 
dendrite position in these two subtypes was used, along with a linear 
regression classifier, to completely split the MG population into MG+ 
and MG− types (Extended Data Table 1 and Extended Data Fig. 2d). We 
extended the subtype morphological characterization of MG cells to 
SG cells as well (Extended Data Table 1). The basal dendrites of SG− 
cells arborize in the ganglion layer (similar to MG−), whereas the basal 
dendrites of SG+ cells arborize in the plexiform layer (similar to MG+). 
Like MG cells, the axons of SG− and SG+ cells have the opposite layer 
pattern as their dendrites: SG− branch predominantly in the plexiform 
layer, whereas SG+ branch in the ganglion layer (Extended Data Fig. 2e). 
Therefore, the relationship between axon and basal dendrite position in 
these two subtypes is also anti-correlated and could be used in a linear 
regression model to split the SG population into SG+ and SG− subtypes 
(Extended Data Fig. 2f).
Other cell types that we classified in this study are the EAFs, the granu-
lar (Gr+) cell, the small plexiform (SP−) cell and GC axons (Fig. 1d,h, 
Extended Data Table 1 and Extended Data Figs. 2g–i and 3a). Granular 
cells (Gr+) are the most numerous cell type in the ELL (apart from the 
GCs of the overlying eminentia granularis posterior)12,13. Despite their 
similar names, only the latter give rise to the GC axons in the molecu-
lar layer. Gr+ cells have small soma and their axons extend towards 
the plexiform and ganglion layers, branching throughout both. Basal 
dendrites of Gr+ ramify through the granular layer of ELL in which EAFs 
terminate. If any apical dendrite exists, it is smooth and constrained 
to the granular layer as well. SP− cells have similarly small soma to Gr+ 
cells, but their somas are mostly in the plexiform layer. The axon of 
SP− cells, after exiting the soma, splits into two branches that go in 
opposite directions: one branch goes towards (and often into) the 
molecular layer with synaptic boutons terminating in both the ganglion 
and molecular layers, and another branch goes towards the granular 
layer with synaptic boutons terminating locally within the plexiform 
layer and sometimes in the granular layer (Extended Data Fig. 2h,i). The 
apical dendrite of SP− cells is variable length (sometimes extending 
into the molecular layer and sometimes constrained to the plexiform 
layer), but is always smooth. GC axons were identified as thin, unmy-
elinated fibres that travelled for long distances in the molecular layer, 
forming excitatory synapses onto molecular layer spines, and entered 
and exited the volume at both faces without having a soma within the 
volume. Reconstructions that did not fit into one of the above catego-
ries, including neuronal processes for which no soma in the volume 
could be identified, were termed ‘unclassified’ .
Spine count estimates
First, we estimated spine density for each of the spiny cell types (Output, 
MG and SG). Following reconstruction and proofreading, five exam-
ple cells of each type were selected for spine annotation. Annotation 
spheres with a radius of 10 µm were placed along apical dendrites of 
these cells and annotation points were manually placed on all spines 
within the sphere. Combined, these annotations enabled estimates of 
spine density (spine count per unit dendrite length) per cell type along 
the extent of the apical dendritic tree. Second, we estimated apical 
dendritic length for each of the same three cell types (Output, MG and 
SG). We densely reconstructed all soma with spiny apical dendrites in a 
60 × 80 × 60 µm region covering the ganglion, plexiform and granular 
layers around the middle of the volume. Then, for each cell with a soma 
in that region, we collected all apical dendrite base segments into a 
single mesh that we then skeletonized (using Igneous; https://github.
com/seung-lab/igneous). The apical dendrite skeletons, combined 
with spine density estimates enabled estimates of the mean total spine 
count per cell of each type.
Synapse identification and annotation
Following training in synapse identification (from M.D.P.), K.E.P., M.G. 
and N.B.S. manually identified and recorded synapses using Neurog-
lancer point annotations using eCREST. Synapses were identifiable 
based on several key features: (1) the presence of a synaptic cleft, char-
acterized by a thickened membrane gap at the junction between an axon 
bouton and a postsynaptic neuronal compartment, which is consist-
ently observed across multiple tissue sections; and (2) the presence of 
an active zone, indicated by the clustering of synaptic vesicles at the 
presynaptic membrane adjacent to the apposition site. Separate point 
annotation classes in Neuroglancer (implemented using eCREST) were 
used to record the location of synapses compared with from a recon-
structed neuron. Presynaptic synapse annotations were placed on the 
presynaptic side of each identified synapse and on a base segment such 
that the base segment associated with that synapse would be recorded 
in the reconstruction file. Postsynaptic synapse annotations were 
placed on the postsynaptic side of each identified synapse. For each 
annotated synapse, either the annotated base segment was matched

with an existing reconstruction containing that base segment, or a new 
reconstruction was seeded and completed from that base segment if it 
could not be found in any existing reconstruction (and subsequently 
merged if resulting in a duplicate or overlapping reconstruction). After 
all synapse base segments were accounted for in the dataset, we could 
create connectivity matrices among reconstructed cells.
Conditional output–input analysis
We implemented the conditional input analysis methods described in 
ref. 61 (see also refs. 62,63). We constructed a connectivity matrix in 
which we counted the total synaptic connections between each post- and 
presynaptic cell and each pre- and postsynaptic cell type (for conditional 
input–output analysis, respectively). Conditional input (associated with 
Figs. 2g,h and 3f,g and Extended Data Fig. 5c): on the condition that a cell 
gets one or more synapses from one cell type A (condition), we counted 
the number of synapses that cell gets from another cell type B (input) 
(less the synapse from cell type A that qualifies the cell for that condi-
tion). This calculation was repeated for all possible pairs of presynaptic 
cell types. Because not all presynaptic sites were annotated for each 
postsynaptic cell, we normalized the connectivity matrix for each cell to 
the fraction of total input from each cell type (probability = number of 
synapses from each presynaptic cell type divided by the total number of 
synapses across all presynaptic cell types). Conditional output (associ-
ated with Extended Data Fig. 6a): on the condition that a cell makes one or 
more synapses to one cell type A (condition), we counted the number of 
synapses that cell makes to another cell type B (output) (less the synapse 
to cell type A that qualifies the cell for that condition). This calculation 
was repeated for all possible pairs of postsynaptic cell types. Because not 
all axons were fully reconstructed for each MG cell (due to axons exiting 
the volume), we normalized the connectivity matrix for each condition 
to the fraction of total synapses to each cell type (probability = number 
of synapses to each postsynaptic cell type divided by the total number 
of synapses across all postsynaptic cell types). Comparison to random 
connectivity: for each set of post- and presynaptic cells, the mean condi-
tional input–output matrix was normalized to a randomized null model 
(Extended Data Figs. 5c and 6a) resulting in z-score normalized values. 
A null model was generated by randomly reassigning pre- and postsyn-
aptic cells for each post- and presynapse in the original connectivity 
matrix for each analysed set of cells (which contains a subset of the cells 
reconstructed for the whole study). In this way, the number of synapses 
each cell gets or makes remains constant, but the post- and presynaptic 
partner identities are randomized (according to a normal distribution 
model). In this way, we generated 100 null models to estimate a distribu-
tion (normal) of randomized conditional input–output probabilities. 
For each pair of input–output conditions, we computed a z-score (that 
is, how many standard deviations the observed connection probability 
is from the mean of the null distribution). The resultant conditional 
input–output matrix could be used to determine whether observed 
connectivity from or to a specific cell type was significantly more or less 
dependent on connectivity from a second input–output than would be 
expected in a random model of connectivity.
Electrophysiological recordings
Neural data presented in Fig. 5 is a re-analysis of data collected in a 
previous study8. Figure 6 includes additional extracellular record -

Briefly, fish were anesthetized (MS:222, 1:25,000) and held against a 
foam pad. Skin on the dorsal surface of the head was removed and a 
long-lasting local anaesthetic (0.75% bupivacaine) was applied to the 
wound margins. A plastic rod was cemented to the anterior portion of 
the skull to secure the head. The posterior portion of the skull overly-

at the end of the surgery (about 20 µg cm−1 of body length), and the 
anaesthetic was removed. Aerated water was passed over the gills of the 
fish for respiration. Paralysis blocks the effect of electromotoneurons 
on the electric organ, preventing the EOD, but the motor command 
signal that would normally elicit an EOD continues to be emitted at 
a rate of 3–5 Hz. The EOD motor command signal (the synchronized 
volley of electromotoneurons that would normally elicit an EOD) was 
recorded with an electrode placed over the electric organ. Extracellular 
single-unit recordings were made from the VLZ using glass microelec-
trodes (2–10 MΩ) filled with 2 M NaCl. For in vivo whole-cell recordings 
electrodes (8–15 MΩ) were filled with an internal solution containing, 
in mM: K-gluconate (122); KCl (7); HEPES (10); Na2GTP (0.4); MgATP 
(4); EGTA (0.5), and 0.5–1% biocytin (pH 7.2, 280–290 mOsm). No cor-
rection was made for liquid junction potentials. Only cells with stable 
membrane potentials more hyperpolarized than −45 mV and spike 
amplitudes >40 mV were analysed. Membrane potentials were recorded 
and filtered at 3–10 kHz (Axoclamp 2B amplifier, Axon Instruments) 
and digitized at 20 kHz (CED micro1401 hardware and Spike2 software; 
Cambridge Electronics Design). For pairing experiments shown in 
Fig. 6, neural responses to the EOD motor command were recorded 
before, during and after 4 min of pairing, in which an EOD mimic was 
presented 4.5 ms following EOD command onset. The EOD mimic was 
a 0.2 ms duration square pulse delivered between an electrode in the 
stomach and another positioned near the electric organ in the tail. 
The amplitude was 25 µA at the output of the stimulus isolation unit 
(stomach electrode negative). Conspecific EOD mimics delivered in 
the experiments shown in Fig. 6 consisted of 0.2 ms duration square 
pulse delivered at a 16.5 ms delay following the EOD motor command 
with amplitudes and polarities that were varied on each command 
(±10 µA, 25 µA, 25 µA and 50 µA) to simulate conspecifics at different 
distances and relative locations.
ELL model
We use a quasi-static approximation to compute cell responses to each 
EOD, and these then evolve across EODs (denoted by an index i) because 
of synaptic plasticity. In the experimental setup we simulate, the aver-
age EOD rate is 4 Hz, so a 4 min period of EOD learning corresponds to 
4 min × 60 s min−1 × 4 EOD s−1 = 960 EODs. Previous experimental and 
modelling work indicates that the mechanisms for sensory cancella-
tion in the ELL generalize across EOD rates, including the substantially 
higher EOD rates used by the fish during foraging64.
We define S as the EOD-generated stimulus to be cancelled, which is 
a decaying sinusoidal function that we fit to the shape of the average 
EOD-evoked stimulus in the data. The amplitude of S is adjusted to 
produce a 2.25 mV change in the membrane potential to match the data 
(Extended Data Fig. 7). We track S and the different model activities for 
a time period from 10 ms before the EOD command to 150 ms after it 
in time steps of 1 ms. Thus, S is a T = 160 component vector across this 
time span. GC activity in the model was taken from in vivo intracellular 
recordings of GC response to EOD corollary discharge input14, and the 
ratio of distributions of GC synapses onto MG, Output and SG cells 
were obtained from the connectome (Extended Data Fig. 3b). The GC 
activity matrix is of dimension T ×  N, where N = 2,088 for Output cells, 
N = 6,390 for MG cells and, when SG cells are included, N = 195 for them. 
Except for Extended Data Fig. 4e, we did not include learning at GC–SG 
synapses as the model shows minimal contribution to the speed of 
cancellation from these synapses (Discussion).

Learning in the noise context
Conspecific EOD echoes were modelled using the EOD waveform 
described in the previous section (S), delayed by 12 ms and with an 
amplitude scaled by 0.75. Echo polarity was randomly assigned as either 
+1 or −1. For the random-noise condition (Extended Data Fig. 8e), the 
noise consisted of about 16 Hz presentations of the EOD waveform deliv-
ered at random times, rendering them temporally uncorrelated with 
granule-cell corollary discharge inputs. Noise amplitudes were drawn 
from a uniform distribution between −1 and 1, where 1 corresponds to 
the self-EOD and −1 corresponds to an EOD of equal magnitude but 
opposite polarity. Initial weights were set such that activity in the MG 
and Output cells perfectly cancelled the self-EOD signal.
Study design
No statistical methods were used to predetermine the sample size for 
the EM connectome analysis. A single EM volume (450 × 450 × 105 µm) 
was generated from one specimen of Gnathonemus petersii, yielding a 
sparsely reconstructed network of 5,025 cells with 29,694 annotated 
synapses. The volume size was chosen based on the known spatial extent 
of the ELL microcircuit and practical limits of serial-section EM acquisi-
tion. For electrophysiological recordings, sample sizes (n = 20–55 cells 
per cell type) were chosen based on previous publications using the 
same preparation and are consistent with the field. No randomization 
was performed. For the EM connectome analysis, cell-type classification 
was performed using morphological criteria (soma location, dendrite 
and axon-layer distributions), which were defined a priori and applied 
consistently. Synapse annotation was performed without knowledge 
of pre- or postsynaptic identity. For electrophysiological recordings, 
no formal blinding was applied, as cell identity was determined during 
recording by anatomical and physiological criteria. Quantification 
of cancellation was performed using standardized analysis pipelines 
applied uniformly across cell types and conditions.

integral for data visualization in all figures visualizing quantitative 
results. Modelling experiments were written and performed in Matlab 
(2024b, Mathworks).
